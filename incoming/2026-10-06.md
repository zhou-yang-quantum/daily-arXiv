## arXiv-2026-10-06 — Item 1

### Realizing Braided Fusion 2-Categories as \((3+1)\)D Mixed-State Topological Orders
**Ryohei Kobayashi, Abhinav Prem, Matthew Yu — arXiv:2610.03864**  
**Priority: must read**

This is today's standout.

In \(2+1\)D, topological order is naturally organized by anyons and **braided fusion categories**. In \(3+1\)D, the excitations include both particles and extended loops, so the corresponding algebraic structure becomes a **braided fusion 2-category**.

The authors claim a remarkably general realization theorem: **every braided fusion 2-category can occur as the topological-operator category of a \(3+1\)D mixed state**. They construct these states using decoherence followed by strong and weak gauging, and give explicit lattice models showing how the categorical data is physically encoded. 

The intrinsically mixed-state orders are diagnosed through **strong higher-form symmetries and their anomalies**. Most interestingly, \(3+1\)D allows something with no \(2+1\)D counterpart: an intrinsically mixed-state phase can have **nondegenerate braiding of topological operators and nevertheless possess a gravitational anomaly** that prevents the same structure from appearing in any \(3+1\)D gapped pure ground state. 

**Background.** Ordinary \(0\)-form symmetry acts on local operators. A \(p\)-form symmetry acts on \(p\)-dimensional extended objects. In mixed states, one must additionally distinguish **strong symmetry**—respected branch-by-branch by the density matrix—from weaker invariance of the averaged density operator. An anomalous strong symmetry can therefore encode information unavailable to a pure-state topological phase.

**Why it matters for you:** very high. Your recent mixed-state work has focused on \(2+1\)D modular data. This paper asks what replaces that structure one dimension higher and, more fundamentally, **which categorical topological data can exist only because the state is mixed**.

The claim that *every* braided fusion 2-category is realizable is strong enough that I would concentrate first on the construction and on precisely what equivalence notion they use for mixed-state topological order.

## arXiv-2026-10-06 — Item 2

### Exact Temporal Criticalities of Entanglement and Negativity in Gaussian and Non-Gaussian Dissipative Kitaev Chains
**Shrey Chowdhary, Sanket Chirame, Stephan Haas, Rishabh Jha — arXiv:2610.03889**  
**Priority: must read; best exact open-system paper**

This introduces a useful kind of dynamical transition that does **not** show up as a singularity in the ordinary entropy.

Take the entanglement spectrum of a subsystem. As the open system evolves, its two largest eigenvalues can cross. Equivalently, the **entanglement Hamiltonian gap closes**, even though \(S_A(t)\) remains perfectly smooth. The authors call this a **temporal criticality**. They define an analogous transition using the negativity spectrum. 

For Gaussian fermionic states they obtain exact criteria. The entanglement gap closes precisely when the subsystem's two fermion-parity sectors have equal weight. The negativity gap closes when this happens for either the subsystem or its complement. Consequently,

\[
t_{\mathcal N}\leq t_E:
\]

the negativity criticality can never occur later than the entanglement criticality. Balanced loss and gain merely shrink the parity imbalance and therefore preserve both criticalities **at arbitrary dissipation strength for any quadratic Hamiltonian**. 

The non-Gaussian comparison is especially valuable. Density dephasing respects parity in every quantum jump but destroys Gaussianity. At a commuting point of the Kitaev chain they solve this dynamics exactly and find that the Gaussian parity criterion fails: weak dephasing moves the spectral crossings away from parity balance; near critical damping the crossings retreat to infinite time; at strong dephasing the negativity begins decreasing long before either spectral gap closes. 

**Background.** Entropy compresses the entire entanglement spectrum into one number:
\[
S=-\sum_i\lambda_i\log\lambda_i.
\]
A level crossing between the largest \(\lambda_i\)'s can therefore represent a qualitative reorganization of the dominant entanglement structure while leaving \(S(t)\) analytic.

**Why it matters for you:** this is almost tailor-made for your Majorana/Gaussian interests. More broadly, it isolates a distinction worth keeping:

\[
\boxed{\text{symmetry structure}\neq\text{Gaussian structure}.}
\]

The exact parity rule follows from Gaussianity, not merely from parity conservation.

[arXiv 2610.03889](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.03889)

## arXiv-2026-10-06 — Item 3

### Magic Quantum Code Surgery
**Kathleen Chang, Anasuya Lyons, Yuanjie Ren, Harald Putterman, Nathanan Tantivasadakarn, Victor V. Albert, Benjamin J. Brown, Dominic J. Williamson — arXiv:2610.03920**  
**Priority: must read; best QEC paper**

This is a substantial 105-page QEC paper, and the basic idea is elegant.

Fault-tolerant universal computation requires non-Clifford logical operations. One route is to realize them by **measuring suitable logical Clifford operators**. The authors give a general method for performing such measurements in **arbitrary QLDPC codes** through a generalized form of code surgery. 

Start from the original QLDPC code and attach ancillary qubits. Then **deform the stabilizer code** so that a product of local stabilizers measures the desired transversal logical Clifford operation.

The important theorem is that this surgery does not sacrifice the desirable asymptotic properties:

\[
\text{LDPC}\longrightarrow\text{LDPC},
\]

while the code distance is reduced by at most a constant factor independent of system size. More strongly, the **fault distance of the logical measurement grows linearly with the original code distance**, including both data and measurement errors. 

They then use these Clifford measurements to implement logical non-Clifford gates and encoded magic-state preparation.

**Background.** Ordinary lattice surgery merges and splits surface-code patches so that products such as \(Z_LZ_L\) become temporarily represented by local stabilizer measurements. This paper generalizes the same philosophy far beyond geometrically local surface codes: change the **code itself temporarily** so that the desired logical operator becomes locally measurable.

**Why it matters for you:** most qLDPC breakthroughs answer “how do we store quantum information efficiently?” Universal computation introduces a second question: **how do we actually manipulate it fault tolerantly without destroying the LDPC advantage?** This paper addresses exactly that interface.

[arXiv 2610.03920](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.03920)

## arXiv-2026-10-06 — Item 4

### How to distinguish chaos from integrability using OTOC
**Alexey Milekhin, Anders Wallberg — arXiv:2610.04197**  
**Priority: extremely high; best integrability/chaos paper**

A standard slogan says that a small late-time OTOC indicates quantum chaos. But that is not actually enough: **interacting integrable systems scramble local operators too**, and their OTOCs can also decay to tiny values.

The authors propose looking instead at a closely related quantity, **operator purity**, and specifically at how its late-time saturation value scales with system size. That scaling separates chaotic from integrable dynamics. 

The analytic mechanism is the **Mazur bound**. Integrable systems possess an extensive family of local or quasilocal conserved charges \(Q_n\). The overlap of an evolving operator with these charges places a lower bound on its surviving long-time component. What matters is not simply “many conserved quantities” but their **locality**: local charges retain parametrically more operator information than the highly nonlocal spectral projectors that formally commute with every Hamiltonian. 

**Background.** The Mazur inequality schematically says
\[
\overline{\langle A(t)A\rangle}
\geq
\sum_n
\frac{|\langle A Q_n\rangle|^2}
{\langle Q_n^2\rangle}.
\]
It is traditionally used to establish finite Drude weights and ballistic transport. Here essentially the same logic is repurposed as a **scrambling diagnostic**.

**Why it matters for you:** this is a useful correction to an increasingly loose use of OTOCs. Integrability does not mean “no scrambling.” The meaningful distinction lies in **how much local information survives and how that remnant scales with \(L\)**.

[arXiv 2610.04197](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.04197)

## arXiv-2026-10-06 — Item 5

### On Information Propagation in Krylov Subspaces
**Pablo Martinez-Azcona, Maximilian Meyer-Moelleringhof, Adolfo del Campo, Apollonas S. Matsoukas-Roubeas — arXiv:2610.03883**  
**Priority: extremely high**

Last week we saw the operational idea of learning a many-body system through its Krylov chain. Today's paper asks a more fundamental question:

**what is the causal structure of that fictitious chain?**

Starting from
\[
|O_0),\quad
\mathcal L|O_0),\quad
\mathcal L^2|O_0),\ldots,
\]
Lanczos orthogonalization converts the dynamics into a one-dimensional hopping model with coefficients \(b_n\). The authors construct a time-ordered path representation in which probability repeatedly branches among Krylov trajectories and derive a **speed limit for information propagation in Krylov space**. 

If
\[
b_n\sim n^\delta,
\]
the Krylov light cone is superballistic for \(\delta>0\); at the maximal allowed growth,
\[
b_n\sim n,
\]
the light cone spreads **exponentially**. Conversely, demanding causal propagation in the Krylov chain implies that the Lanczos coefficients cannot grow faster than linearly. 

They demonstrate the result analytically for \(SL(2,\mathbb R)\) and Heisenberg-Weyl algebras and numerically in a skew-field Ising model, then formulate a Lieb-Robinson analogue by mapping the problem to a spin chain.

**Background.** Spatial Lieb-Robinson bounds constrain *where* an operator can spread. Krylov complexity instead measures how far it spreads into increasingly complicated nested commutators. These are different geometries:
\[
\text{real-space distance}\quad\leftrightarrow\quad
\text{operator-complexity distance}.
\]

**Why it matters for you:** this gives a concrete dynamical meaning to the frequently quoted “linear Lanczos growth” conjecture for chaotic systems. Linear \(b_n\) is not just a convenient empirical form—it corresponds to the **fastest causal exponential propagation permitted in Krylov space**.

[arXiv 2610.03883](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.03883)

## arXiv-2026-10-06 — Item 6

### Driven-Dissipative Ground State Preparation: Mixing Time and Randomness
**Paul Cazeaux, Marius Junge, Diyi Liu — arXiv:2610.03860**  
**Priority: extremely high; best quantum-algorithm/open-system paper**

Most dissipative ground-state-preparation algorithms choose one carefully engineered time-independent Lindbladian. This paper instead asks whether **continually changing randomized dissipation** can robustly cool an arbitrary state.

The jump operators are constructed by taking random matrices and averaging their Heisenberg evolution under the target Hamiltonian. Repeated empirical averages of the resulting Lindbladians preferentially remove high-energy components and drive **arbitrary initial density matrices toward low energy**, so the method does not require a warm start or substantial initial ground-state overlap. 

A notable structural result is that their mixing-time bounds depend on the Hamiltonian principally through its **eigenvalue distribution**, rather than detailed eigenvectors. Randomness effectively washes out much of the microscopic basis dependence. 

**Background.** Filtering a random operator \(A\) by
\[
A_f=\int dt\,f(t)e^{iHt}Ae^{-iHt}
\]
weights its matrix element \(A_{mn}\) by the Fourier transform \(\tilde f(E_m-E_n)\). Appropriate filters can therefore suppress transitions that increase energy and retain those that decrease it. Random \(A\)'s ensure broad connectivity between energy levels.

**Why it matters for you:** it complements several recent dissipative-preparation papers from a different direction. Instead of exploiting geometric locality, this paper asks how much can be guaranteed from **spectral information plus randomness alone**.

[arXiv 2610.03860](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.03860)

## arXiv-2026-10-06 — Item 7

### Generalized lattices for quantum error correction
**arXiv:2610.04723**  
**Priority: very high; QEC broadening pick**

This paper develops a common stabilizer formalism for **finite-dimensional qudits, bosonic oscillators, and planar rotors**.

The unifying object is a generalized lattice in quantum phase space. Its commuting generators may contain ordinary qudit Paulis, oscillator displacements, and rotor displacements simultaneously. The authors construct genuinely hybrid **oscillator-rotor, rotor-qudit, and oscillator-rotor-qubit codes** that cannot be separated into independent constituent codes by generalized Clifford transformations. 

The formalism is impressively unified. Logical operators come from the **symplectic dual lattice**; the logical dimension equals the lattice covolume; Clifford-Gaussian logical gates arise from lattice symmetries; and the same machinery yields notions of distance, optimal decoding and quantum weight enumerators. Known Pauli, qudit and GKP MacWilliams identities all emerge as special cases. 

They also construct an oscillator-qubit code by partially applying Construction A to the classical Golay code and show how to produce finite-energy approximate codewords with exponentially small encoding error.

**Background.** A GKP code is fundamentally a **lattice code in continuous phase space**. Stabilizer codes over qudits are also discrete symplectic-lattice constructions. This paper takes that analogy literally and puts them into one algebraic framework.

**Why it matters for you:** it is a useful broadening beyond surface codes. More conceptually, it suggests that discrete-variable and continuous-variable QEC are not merely analogous—they are different realizations of the **same symplectic lattice geometry**.

## arXiv-2026-10-06 — Item 8

### Complex Worldline Instantons in (Anti-)de Sitter Space
**Alberto Castellano, Jinwei Chu, Eliseu Kloster, Jamie A. P. Law-Smith — arXiv:2610.03862**  
**Priority: very high; best conventional QFT broadening paper**

This is my deliberate QFT selection outside quantum information.

The worldline formalism rewrites a one-loop field-theory determinant as a path integral over closed particle trajectories. Nonperturbative phenomena such as **Schwinger pair production** are then controlled semiclassically by special closed trajectories—worldline instantons.

The authors show that in curved spacetime the relevant saddles generically require complexifying **both spacetime coordinates and proper time**. Rather than treating the resulting real- and imaginary-proper-time solutions separately, they show that they form two slices of one complex family. 

The imaginary-proper-time branch supplies the familiar exponentially suppressed factor,
\[
\Gamma\sim e^{-S_{\rm inst}},
\]
while the real-proper-time branch can generate complex phases and therefore **interference between nonperturbative saddles**. They classify the topologically inequivalent instantons through winding numbers around compact directions and punctures, and rewrite their actions in terms of residues of a complexified conjugate momentum. 

In AdS an especially clean parameter appears:
\[
\frac{R|eE|}{m},
\]
separating sub-extremal, extremal and super-extremal saddle structures.

**Background.** This is essentially steepest-descent/resurgence physics applied not to an ordinary integral but to a **functional integral over particle trajectories**. Complex saddles need not correspond to real classical paths; nevertheless they can dominate real physical amplitudes.

**Why it matters for you:** it is a good concrete example of complex saddle geometry in QFT, quite separate from your recent error-correction work, and provides useful intuition for instantons, Stokes phenomena and nonperturbative semiclassics.

## arXiv-2026-10-06 — Item 9

### Efficient Quantum Monte Carlo through Cluster Expansions
**Jorge Sánchez-Segovia, Álvaro M. Alhambra — arXiv:2610.03893**  
**Priority: very high; statistical-mechanics/algorithms pick**

Cluster expansions give rigorous polynomial-time classical algorithms for quantum partition functions at sufficiently high temperature, but there is an awkward computational bottleneck: one normally has to **enumerate every connected cluster**.

For an interaction graph of degree \(\Delta\), that creates an overhead roughly
\[
(N/\epsilon)^{O(\log\Delta)}.
\]
For long-range interactions, where effectively \(\Delta\sim N\), this becomes quasipolynomial. 

The authors replace exhaustive enumeration with a Markov chain that **Monte Carlo samples directly in the abstract space of connected clusters**. Importantly, this is not ordinary worldline/path-integral Monte Carlo. Within the convergence regime of the cluster expansion, the notorious physical sign problem does not reappear.

They prove an FPRAS for general polymer models with roughly
\[
\widetilde O(N^4\epsilon^{-3})
\]
runtime and improve the scaling for quantum lattice partition functions. The construction applies directly to long-range interactions
\[
J(r)\sim r^{-\alpha}
\]
for suitable \(\alpha\), including two-body systems with \(\alpha>2D\). 

**Background.** A cluster expansion reorganizes
\[
\log Z
\]
as a sum over **connected** interaction clusters. Convergence at high temperature means large clusters are exponentially suppressed. The new idea is to sample those suppressed combinatorial objects rather than enumerate them.

**Why it matters for you:** this is a nice example where a Monte Carlo method avoids the quantum sign problem because it samples **terms in an analytic expansion rather than quantum configurations**. That distinction is broadly useful when thinking about what the sign problem does—and does not—rule out.

## arXiv-2026-10-06 — Item 10

### Sum-of-squares compilation of matrix-model dynamics for NISQ and fault-tolerant hardware
**Ayanendu Dutta, Nabanita Sarkar — arXiv:2610.04731**  
**Priority: high; quantum-simulation sleeper pick**

The target is the matrix-model interaction

\[
V=-\frac{g^2}{4}\sum_{I,J}
\operatorname{Tr}[X_I,X_J]^2,
\]

which appears in BFSS/BMN-type matrix quantum mechanics and is notoriously expensive to compile digitally. A straightforward phase-polynomial implementation costs approximately \(O(d^2N^4Q^4)\) rotations per Trotter step. 

The authors first prove something useful: within a broad conventional gate class, the **Walsh support of the potential gives a lower bound** on both rotation and two-qubit gate counts, and an optimized parity network nearly saturates it. So merely finding a cleverer circuit inside that gate family cannot produce a large asymptotic improvement.

The escape is to exploit the exact algebraic structure
\[
-\operatorname{Tr}[X_I,X_J]^2
=
\sum_a
(\text{commutator component}_a)^2.
\]

For NISQ hardware they coherently accumulate each component into a Fourier register and square it there, reducing two-qubit cost to \(O(d^2N^3Q^3)\). For fault-tolerant hardware, reversible arithmetic plus phase-gradient injection gives \(O(d^2N^3Q^2)\) \(T\) gates. Their ablations show that the gain comes from the **sum-of-squares factorization itself**, not simply from switching to arithmetic circuits. 

**Background.** Quantum simulation cost can depend dramatically on the *algebraic representation* of the same Hamiltonian. Expanding a polynomial into thousands of Pauli phases may destroy structure that an arithmetic circuit can exploit directly.

**Why it matters for you:** the general lesson is stronger than the matrix-model application:
\[
\boxed{\text{compile the mathematical structure, not merely the expanded Hamiltonian}.}
\]
This is precisely the kind of quantum-algorithm work I think is worth including despite excluding most heavily CS-oriented circuit-optimization papers.
